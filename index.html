The physics

What you're looking at

A Schwarzschild black hole of mass M: no spin, no charge, just curved spacetime around a point. Everything here is in geometric units with G = c = M = 1, so a distance of “6 M” means six gravitational radii, and one unit of time is the time light takes to cross one of them. Nothing is a texture or a pre-baked image. For every pixel, every frame, the GPU fires a light ray backwards from the camera and integrates its path as a null geodesic of the Schwarzschild metric until it either hits the disk, falls through the horizon, or escapes to the stars.

The shadow and the photon ring

The event horizon sits at r = 2M. Slightly outside it, at r = 3M, is the photon sphere: the radius at which light can orbit the hole in a circle. Any ray that dips inside 3M on its way in cannot come back out, so the hole casts a “shadow” that is larger than the horizon itself. Its apparent radius is √27 M ≈ 5.2 M, more than two and a half times the horizon radius. The thin, brilliant ring hugging the edge of the shadow is the photon ring: light from the disk and the sky that looped around the hole once, twice, or more before escaping toward you. Each extra loop adds a thinner, fainter ring just inside the last.

Why the disk wraps over the top

A flat disk seen nearly edge-on should hide its far half behind the hole. Instead you see it arched above and below the shadow. That is gravitational lensing: light from the far side of the disk is bent over the north pole of the hole to reach you, and light from the underside of the far disk is bent under the south pole. When the camera lines up exactly with the hole and a background source, the bent paths close into a full circle, an Einstein ring; the arches you see here are the partial version of the same effect, drawn by the disk instead of a distant star.

Why one side is brighter

Gas at the inner edge of the disk, the ISCO at r = 6M, circles the hole at about half the speed of light. At that speed relativistic Doppler beaming is not subtle. The side of the disk moving toward you is brightened and shifted blue; the side receding is dimmed and shifted red. Because the observed intensity scales as g4, where g is the ratio of received to emitted frequency, a modest shift becomes a dramatic brightness asymmetry. Switch Doppler beaming off and the disk becomes a symmetric, rather boring, ring.

Gravitational redshift

Light that climbs out of the potential well loses energy. For gas in a circular orbit the combined effect of gravity and orbital time dilation gives g = √(1 − 3M/r) as seen from far away, so emission from the inner disk arrives dimmer and redder than its true temperature would suggest; at the ISCO it has lost about 30% of its frequency. Move the camera in close and the opposite happens: a static observer deep in the well sees the whole universe blueshifted and brightened, by a factor 1/√(1 − 2M/r).

What's approximated

The disk is infinitesimally thin and geometrically flat, with a Novikov–Thorne temperature profile and a blackbody colour. The spacetime is Schwarzschild, not Kerr: there is no spin, no frame dragging, no lopsided shadow. There is no gas physics or radiative transfer, no self-shadowing, no magnetic fields, and the turbulence is procedural noise rather than magnetohydrodynamics. The stars and the background glow are procedural too. What is not approximated is the light bending: every ray is integrated through the exact Schwarzschild geometry.
